TournamentIQ — Predicting Match Outcomes with the Poisson Distribution
TournamentIQ is an intelligent system that tracks team strength using live 2026 FIFA World Cup data. This article discusses how I used the Poisson distribution to predict match outcomes.
1. What is Poisson, and why does it fit football
Poisson is a probability distribution used to model the number of discrete events over a fixed interval. A football match is a fixed interval. Goals are discrete events. That makes Poisson a natural fit for predicting how many goals a team will score.
I am using it here as the baseline prediction engine for TournamentIQ, my live 2026 FIFA World Cup intelligence system. The model runs before every match and produces win, draw, and loss probabilities based on what has happened in the tournament so far.
The formula is:
P(k) = (λ^k × e^−λ) / k!
Where:
* λ (lambda) is the expected number of goals for a team in this specific match. The model derives lambda from each team's recent stats, their opponent's defensive record, and their relative Elo rating. It is not a fixed number.
* k is the number of goals you are asking the probability for. I run the formula once for k=0, once for k=1, once for k=2, and so on.
* e is Euler's number, 2.718. It appears here as a decay factor. It ensures that as k increases, the probabilities eventually shrink toward zero rather than growing without limit.
* k! is k factorial. 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6. It accounts for the number of ways k goals can be arranged within the match.
If the model predicts a team will score 1.5 goals on average, Poisson tells you the probability they score exactly 0, exactly 1, exactly 2, and so on.
2. How the model produces win, draw, and loss probabilities
I run Poisson for both teams independently. One lambda for the home team, one for the away team. That produces a probability for every possible scoreline up to a defined ceiling. Every cell in the matrix is the product of two independent Poisson probabilities.
P(home scores h, away scores a) = P(λ_home, h) × P(λ_away, a)
I then sum across the matrix in three groups:
All cells where h > a → home win probability
All cells where h = a → draw probability
All cells where h < a → away win probability


One limitation is built into this independence assumption. Poisson predicts both teams' scores as if nothing one team does affects the other. In reality, a red card, a goal, or a tactical change affects both teams simultaneously. I use this model as a baseline. It captures team strength and historical patterns, but cannot react to events inside the match.

Step 1: Smoothing
We apply smoothing to the Poisson because we have insufficient data. Two matches are not enough data to fully trust. A team that scored 4 in their first match and 0 in their second looks like a 2.0 goals per match team, but that first match might have been a mismatch. The smoothing formula blends each team's actual stats with the tournament average. The more matches played, the more weight is applied to the actual numbers.
The baseline model uses a tournament average of 1.5 goals per match. This is an industry standard figure. Historical tournament averages range from 1.5 to 2.5 goals per team per match.


weight = min(played / 3.0, 1.0)
smoothed = (weight × actual_avg) + ((1 − weight) × TOURNAMENT_AVG_GOALS)


Both teams have played 2 matches, so the weight is the same for both.


weight = min(2 / 3.0, 1.0) = 0.667


66.7% of the smoothed figure comes from the team's actual stats, and 33.3% comes from the tournament average of 1.5 goals per match.


Jordan
smoothed scored   = (0.667 × 1.000) + (0.333 × 1.5) = 0.667 + 0.500 = 1.167
smoothed conceded = (0.667 × 2.500) + (0.333 × 1.5) = 1.668 + 0.500 = 2.168


Argentina
smoothed scored   = (0.667 × 2.500) + (0.333 × 1.5) = 1.668 + 0.500 = 2.168
smoothed conceded = (0.667 × 0.000) + (0.333 × 1.5) = 0.000 + 0.500 = 0.500


Argentina's raw conceded average is 0.000. Smoothing pulls that figure up to 0.500 because two matches carry only 66.7% weight. The model treats Argentina as an elite defence but not an impenetrable one.
Step 2: Attack strength, defence weakness, and Elo factor
The formula needs ratios because it measures how much better or worse each team is compared to a neutral baseline.
Attack strength: how prolific is this team relative to the tournament average?
attack_strength = smoothed_scored / TOURNAMENT_AVG_GOALS


Defence weakness: how easy is the opponent to score against, relative to the tournament average?
defense_weakness = opponent_smoothed_conceded / TOURNAMENT_AVG_GOALS


When calculating Jordan's lambda, Jordan's attack strength combines with Argentina's defence weakness. The defence weakness answers a specific question. How many goals should Jordan score, given how well Jordan attacks and how well Argentina defends?


Elo factor: a final strength adjustment capped between 0.8 and 1.2
elo_factor = max(0.8, min(1.2, team_elo / opponent_elo))


The Elo factor can shift lambda by at most 20% in either direction. A team with a much higher Elo rating gets a boost. A team with a much lower Elo rating gets a penalty.


Calculating Jordan's lambda:
attack_strength  = 1.167 / 1.5 = 0.778
defense_weakness = 0.500 / 1.5 = 0.333   (← Argentina's smoothed conceded)
elo_factor       = max(0.8, min(1.2, 959.82 / 1051.72)) = 0.913
lambda_Jordan    = 1.5 × 0.778 × 0.333 × 0.913 = 0.354


Calculating Argentina's lambda:
attack_strength  = 2.168 / 1.5 = 1.445
defense_weakness = 2.168 / 1.5 = 1.445   (← Jordan's smoothed conceded)
elo_factor       = max(0.8, min(1.2, 1051.72 / 959.82)) = 1.096
lambda_Argentina = 1.5 × 1.445 × 1.445 × 1.096 = 3.433


Argentina's defence weakness of 0.333 is the lowest possible realistic figure after smoothing. It pulls Jordan's lambda down hard. Argentina's high attack strength multiplied by Jordan's high defence weakness multiplied by a favourable Elo factor compounds into a lambda of 3.433. That is a dominant expected goals figure for a single match.


Step 3: Goal probability distributions
Lambda tells us the expected goals for each team. The Poisson formula converts that into a probability for every possible scoreline.


P(k) = (λ^k × e^−λ) / k!

P(0) = (0.354^0 × e^−0.354) / 0! = (1.000 × 0.702) / 1 = 0.702
P(1) = (0.354^1 × e^−0.354) / 1! = (0.354 × 0.702) / 1 = 0.248
P(2) = (0.354^2 × e^−0.354) / 2! = (0.125 × 0.702) / 2 = 0.044
P(3) = (0.354^3 × e^−0.354) / 3! = (0.044 × 0.702) / 6 = 0.005


A lambda of 0.354 produces a tight distribution clustered around 0 and 1. Jordan scores 0 goals 70.2% of the time. By k=3 the probability is 0.005, so I stop there.


Argentina's goal probabilities (lambda = 3.433):
P(0) = (3.433^0 × e^−3.433) / 0! = (1.000 × 0.032) / 1   = 0.032
P(1) = (3.433^1 × e^−3.433) / 1! = (3.433 × 0.032) / 1   = 0.111

Argentina's distribution is spread across 2, 3, and 4 goals. Scoring 2 goals happens 19.1% of the time, 3 goals 21.9%, and 4 goals 18.8%. A lambda of 3.433 means a high-scoring outcome is the most likely result, not the exception. I show probabilities up to k=6 here because at that point the probability is still 7.4%. Argentina scoring 6 is more likely than Jordan scoring 2.

4. Limitations of the baseline model
The baseline model works. But running it across the full 2026 tournament group stage exposed four problems.
The tournament average of 1.5 was not specific to this tournament.
I used 1.5 as the baseline lambda because it is an industry standard figure. Historical tournament averages sit between 1.5 and 2.5 goals per team per match. The actual 2026 FIFA World Cup average came out at 2.938 once each team had played two matches. Every lambda the baseline produced was anchored to a figure nearly half the real tournament rate.
No protection against outlier scorelines
A team that scores 7 in one match and 0 in the next has the same raw average as a team that scores 3 and 4. The baseline model treats them identically. One outlier match pulls a team's strength estimate away from their true level with no correction applied.
No awareness of match context
A team that has already qualified plays differently from a team that must win to stay in the tournament. The baseline model assigns no weight to motivation. Every match carries the same expected intensity regardless of what is at stake.
The scoreline matrix was truncated too early
The baseline model runs up to max_goals = 6 per team. When lambda is very high or very low, meaningful probability mass sits above that ceiling and gets lost. The three outcome probabilities do not sum to 1.0. Three fixtures from the 2026 group stage show the scale of the problem:






Tunisia vs Netherlands:   0.003 + 0.007 + 0.411 = 0.421
Japan vs Sweden:          0.637 + 0.106 + 0.113 = 0.856
Turkey vs United States:  0.020 + 0.091 + 0.867 = 0.978


Tunisia vs the Netherlands is the most severe case. The combined probabilities sum to only 0.421. More than half the probability mass is sitting above the six-goal ceiling unaccounted for. Extreme lambdas amplify the problem because the Poisson distribution for a high lambda team spreads probability across many goal values, not just the first six.
5. The refined model
The four limitations above drove a set of targeted changes. Each change addresses one specific problem in the baseline.
5.1 What changed and why
Global lambda updated from 1.5 to 2.938
The baseline anchored every prediction to a historical guess. I computed the actual 2026 FIFA World Cup average once each team had finished two matches and stored it as GLOBAL_LAMBDA = 2.938. Every lambda calculation now anchors to the real tournament rate.
Winsorization: goals capped at 4 per match
Before averaging, any single match goal total above 4 is capped at 4. A 7-0 win counts as a 4-0 win for the purposes of measuring attack strength. This prevents one outlier match from pulling a team's average away from their true level.
Germany vs Curaçao is the clearest illustration. Germany scored 7 goals. Without the cap, Germany's attack average after one match is 7.0, five times the tournament average. Every subsequent prediction would treat Germany as historically dominant based on one match against the weakest side in the tournament. With the cap, Germany's total score becomes 4. The model still recognises a convincing win. It does not let one scoreline define a team's attacking profile.


Without cap:  Germany attack average = 7.0 / 1 = 7.000
With cap:     Germany attack average = 4.0 / 1 = 4.000


Both tell the model that Germany is a strong attacking team. Only one of them lets a single freak scoreline dominate every future prediction. Curaçao's conceded figure receives the same treatment. Without the cap, they look like a team that cannot defend. With the cap, they look like a team that had a very bad day.
Laplace smoothing replaces the weight blend
The baseline smoothing formula was a manually constructed weight blend with an arbitrary threshold of 3 matches for full trust. Laplace smoothing replaces it with a standard statistical technique that adds phantom matches to every team's record before calculating their factor.
The phantom contribution is 1.5 imaginary matches where the team performed exactly at the tournament average. A team with 2 real matches gets treated as if they played 3.5. A team with 0 matches gets treated as if they played 1.5. The phantom matches pull every team toward the average when data is thin and fade into irrelevance as real matches accumulate.
attack_factor  = (capped_scored + α × λ) / (λ × (played + α))
defense_factor = (capped_conceded + α × λ) / (λ × (played + α))


Where α = 1.5 and λ = 2.938


The result is a ratio. A factor of 1.0 means the team is performing exactly at the tournament average. Above 1.0 means stronger than average. Below 1.0 means weaker than average. A high attack factor is good. A low defence factor is good.
Low score matrix adjustments
The raw Poisson distribution underestimates how often low-scoring results happen in football. Defences organise, teams play conservatively when protecting leads, and 0-0 draws happen more than the math predicts. After building the raw matrix, I apply four empirical corrections drawn from 2026 tournament scoreline frequencies:


P(0,0) × 1.50
P(1,0) × 1.20
P(0,1) × 1.20
P(1,1) × 1.30


The four adjusted cells are now too large relative to the rest of the matrix. The total across all 121 cells no longer sums to 1.0. To bring it back to 1, the entire matrix is normalised by dividing by its new total.
Motivation layer
A team's lambda is adjusted after calculation based on their match situation. Three scenarios are handled:


Home must win, away qualified or eliminated:
    lambda_home × 1.15,  lambda_away × 0.85


Away must win, home qualified or eliminated:
    lambda_away × 1.15,  lambda_home × 0.85


Both teams must win:
    lambda_home × 1.10,  lambda_away × 1.10


All other combinations: no adjustment applied
T
The layer does not fire for every fixture. A match between two already qualified teams, or two already eliminated teams, carries no adjustment. The model treats those matches as equally motivated in both directions.
Jordan vs Argentina illustrates what happens when no asymmetry exists. Jordan was already eliminated going into matchday 3. Argentina had already qualified. Neither team gets a boost or a penalty. The motivation layer does not apply to this fixture. The refined model probabilities come entirely from the Laplace smoothing, the updated global lambda, and the matrix corrections.
The contrast with the worked example in section 5.2.1 is instructive. That example shows the refined model without the motivation layer, so the math stays clean. The full refined model output for Jordan vs Argentina, with all components active, is:
Jordan win: 0.099  |  Draw: 0.189  |  Argentina win: 0.712


Compare that to the worked example without motivation:


Jordan win: 0.038  |  Draw: 0.112  |  Argentina win: 0.850


The difference comes from the motivation tags. Jordan's elimination and Argentina qualified means neither lambda is boosted. But the matrix corrections and the higher global lambda still pull Argentina's win probability down from the baseline's 92.2% to 71.2%.
